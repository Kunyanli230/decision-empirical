SWE-VERIFIER: Selective Verification for Cost-Efficient Coding Agents
Kaijian Zou1* Vaibhav Chennareddy1 Yunxiang Zhang1 Naihao Deng1
Longju Bai1 Jiaxin Pei2 Rada Mihalcea1 Lu Wang1
1University of Michigan 2The University of Texas at Austin
Abstract
Coding agents spend substantial computation
generating actions and processing long inter-
action histories. Poor action choices can steer
agents into low-value states, wasting computa-
tion and increasing API costs. Sampling and
verifying alternative actions can improve the
agent’s decisions, but applying this procedure
at every step adds substantial overhead. We in-
troduce SWE-VERIFIER, a lightweight gener-
ative model that selectively triggers candidate
sampling and verification. It scores the cod-
ing agent’s proposed action to determine when
additional candidate sampling is needed, then
ranks the resulting actions before execution.
We train SWE-VERIFIERfrom Qwen3.5-4B
using preference data that groups contrasting
actions with their resulting environment obser-
vations and rubric-guided teacher scores. Using
the mini-swe-agent harness, SWE-VERIFIER
improves resolution from 75.47% to 77.20%
on MiniMax-M2.7 at 1.09× cost and general-
izes to DeepSeek-V4-Flash without training on
its actions, improving resolution from 72.60%
to 74.27% at 1.13 × cost. Across both mod-
els, SWE-VERIFIERverifies fewer than 4% of
steps and achieves comparable or higher res-
olution than the trajectory and all-step base-
lines at substantially lower cost. These results
demonstrate that selective candidate sampling
and lightweight verification provide comple-
mentary gains in coding-agent performance and
inference efficiency. Our model, code, and
data are available at https://github.com/
zkjzou/SWE-Verifier.

Xia et al., 2025). While the final patch is the ul-

a low-value trajectory, requiring additional explo-

tion of coding-agent costs (Bai et al., 2026; Salim

Improving action quality before execution is there-
fore an opportunity to improve both task success
and inference efficiency.
Verification offers a way to allocate additional
computation to better decisions. Existing test-time
scaling methods apply this idea by adjusting rea-
soning effort or selecting among independently

sampled solutions (Snell et al., 2025; Brown et al.,
2024; Lightman et al., 2024). For coding agents,
outcome-level and trajectory-level verifiers simi-
larly rank completed patches or rollouts (Pan et al.,
2025; Jain et al., 2025; Han et al., 2026; Zainul-

tion after completion cannot prevent an individual
rollout from taking an unproductive branch, and
every candidate trajectory must be generated be-
fore it can be judged. This motivates verification
at intermediate decision points, before the agent
commits to its next action.
Step-level verification samples alternative ac-
tions from the current agent state and uses a verifier
to choose which one to execute (Figure 1).Existing
coding-agent process verifiers often rely on noisy
step labels propagated from final outcomes and ap-
ply verification at every step (Han et al., 2026; Zain-
ullina et al., 2025; Gandhi et al., 2025; Dihan and
Khan, 2026). Applying this procedure at every step,
however, incurs candidate-generation and scoring
overhead throughout the trajectory, typically using
the coding agent itself as the verifier. Not every de-
cision warrants this expense. We therefore studyse-
lective step-level verification: assess the proposed
action first, then generate and compare alternatives
only when additional scrutiny is useful. The cen-
tral challenge isbalancing task accuracy against
compute budget by deciding which coding-agent
actions warrant additional verification.
We formalize step-level action selection and
decompose its policy and verifier costs to com-
pare full and selective verification with trajectory-
level scaling. A preliminary study with MiniMax-
M2.7 (MiniMax, 2026) on SWE-Bench-Verified
(Jimenez et al., 2024) shows that selective verifi-
cation outperforms all-step verification, achieving
similar task performance (76.6% versus 76.0%)
while significantly reduce the compute cost (1.29×
versus 2.32× baseline cost) by verifying only 4%
of steps. These observations motivate two com-
plementary design choices to save computing bud-
get: concentrate candidate comparison at useful
decision points and offload action evaluation to
a lightweight model.
Motivated by these findings, we introduce SWE-
VERIFIER, a framework for selective verification of
coding-agent actions. A single generative verifier
scores the policy’s proposed action to determine
whether to sample alternatives, then scores the re-
sulting candidates to select the action to execute.
We instantiate SWE-VERIFIERwith Qwen3.5-4B
(Qwen Team, 2026) and train it using an execution-
aware preference-data construction recipe. We
sample contrasting actions from models with dif-
ferent capabilities, including MiniMax-M2.7 and
Qwen3.5-27B, at intermediate states drawn from
successful trajectories generated by Opus 4.7 and
GPT-5.5. We replay these actions to obtain en-
vironment feedback and use GPT-5.6 to produce
rubric-guided preference supervision for each can-
didate group during training.
Our experiments show that selective verifica-
tion improves the performance–cost tradeoff across
two policy models. On MiniMax-M2.7, SWE-
VERIFIERraises resolution from 75.47% to 77.20%
while verifying only 3.43% of steps and adding
9% total cost, outperforming trajectory verifica-
tion and both evaluated all-step verifiers. Impor-
tantly, SWE-VERIFIERgeneralizes to DeepSeek-
V4-Flash, improving resolution from 72.60% to
74.27% at 1.13 × total cost. Together, these re-
sults show that selective candidate sampling and
lightweight action verification can improve coding-
agent performance without the overhead of uniform
verification at every step.

3 SWE-VERIFIER: Selective Step-Level
Verification for Coding Agents
The formulation in Section 2 highlights a central
challenge: verification is not uniformly beneficial
across decision states. At some steps, it provides
no gain or can even hurt action selection, while
at others its marginal improvement may not jus-
tify the added candidate-generation and scoring
cost. Our preliminary study with MiniMax-M2.7
3

illustrates this tradeoff (Table 3 in Appendix A).
Full verification reaches 76.0% resolution at 2.32×
total cost, with a substantial portion of the over-
head coming from using the large policy model to
score candidate actions. By contrast, verification
restricted to closeout steps reaches 76.6% resolu-
tion while verifying only 4% of steps at 1.29× cost.
Periodic verification at a fixed frequency does not
provide the same benefit as closeout-only verifica-
tion, showing that effectiveness depends on which
steps are verified. These observations motivate two
complementary ways to reduce verification over-
head: concentrate verification on steps where it is
useful and offload action evaluation to a smaller
model. Full results and experimental details ap-
pear in Appendix A.
Motivated by these findings, we introduce SWE-
VERIFIER, a lightweight generative verifier for se-
lective step-level verification. It scores the policy’s
proposed action to determine whether additional
verification is needed and ranks candidate actions
to determine which one the agent should execute.
Figure 2 summarizes the data construction, train-
ing, and inference procedure.
To our knowledge, SWE-VERIFIERis the first
framework for coding agents to combine selective
step-level verification with a trained generative ac-
tion verifier. We further introduce an execution-
aware construction recipe for its training data: sam-
pling contrasting actions at each trajectory step, re-
playing them to observe their outcomes, and using
a strong teacher to generate rubric-guided prefer-
ence labels for each candidate group.
3.1 Gate and Selector Modes
Selective verification requires two decisions with
different roles: whether the expected value of alter-
natives justifies generating them, and which action
to execute once alternatives are available. We as-
sign these decisions to gate and selector modes,
respectively. Ingate mode, SWE-VERIFIERscores
the policy’s initial actionat; the agent executes a
high-scoring action directly and invokes additional
verification when the score falls below a threshold.
Inselector mode, it scores every action a∈A t in
the candidate group, generating a prediction of each
action’s likely repository effect and task progress
before the agent executes the highest-scoring valid
candidate. Thus, the same verifier score determines
both when to generate alternatives and which ac-
tion to select from them. We use Qwen3.5-4B
(Qwen Team, 2026) as the base model and further
improve it through the preference-data construction
and training procedure described next.
3.2 Preference Data Construction
We construct the training data in four steps.
Step 1: Collect Successful Trajectories.We
use Opus 4.7 and GPT-5.5 as representative strong
coding agents available at the time of our experi-
ments. Together, they provide 500 successful tra-
jectories from the SWE-Smith llm_rewrite and
pr_mirror instance types.
Step 2: Sample and Filter Candidate Actions.
For each step in the collected trajectories, we sam-
ple five actions from weak and strong models to
expose the verifier to contrasting alternatives. We
then filter candidates based on their pairwise Jac-
card similarity,
J(a, b) = |S(a)∩S(b)|
|S(a)∪S(b)| .(6)
Here, S(a) denotes the set of tokens in action a.
We discard candidates whose Jaccard similarity
to an already retained action is at least 0.8. This
prevents the teacher from spending labels on near-
identical actions.
Step 3: Replay Actions for Environment Feed-
back.We replay each retained candidate from its
agent state and record the resulting environment
observation. We remove candidates with duplicate
non-empty environment observations.
Step 4: Teacher Labeling.We label candidate
steps with GPT-5.6. The teacher receives the cur-
rent trajectory prefix, candidate actions, and their
immediate observations. The teacher then gen-
erates structured supervision comprising each ac-
tion’s expected execution outcome, anticipated con-
tribution to task progress, a shared rubric for evalu-
ating the candidate group, and a final score for each
candidate. This supervision teaches the verifier to
assess not only which action is preferable but also
why it is expected to improve the agent state. The
teacher prompt is provided in Appendix D.3, and
Appendix E gives a concrete example of teacher
output and analyzes alternative teacher prompts.

4 Experimental Setup
4.1 Task and Metrics
We evaluate the main verification strategies
on the full 500-task SWE-Bench Verified set
with the mini-swe-agent framework (Yang et al.,
2024), using MiniMax-M2.7 (MiniMax, 2026) and
DeepSeek-V4-Flash (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) as pol-
icy models. The gate/selector and threshold analy-
ses use a 202-task subset. We report task-resolution
rate, average trajectory steps, verify rate, and cost
normalized to the corresponding no-verifier pol-
icy baseline. For cost accounting, we assume zero
inference cost for the local lightweight verifier.
4.2 Verification Settings
Baselines.No verificationexecutes the initially
sampled policy action and defines the 1.00 ×
cost reference.Trajectory verificationsamples
three complete rollouts: For LLM-as-a-Verifier@3
(Kwok et al., 2026), the policy model also serves as
the verifier that selects among three sampled roll-
outs; Oracle@3 gives an upper bound by selecting a
successful rollout whenever one exists.All-step ver-
ificationgenerates three candidate actions at every
step and selects with either the policy model (Self-
Verification) or Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team, 2026).
Selective verification.SWE-VERIFIERscores
the policy’s initial action and triggers additional
candidate sampling when its score falls below a
threshold, then ranks the candidates and selects
the highest-scoring valid action. We evaluate four
variants:Untrained,Score Only,Joint Rubric,
andTwo-Stage Rubric. We use a 202-task subset
with higher outcome variability to identify a cost-
effective threshold for each verifier on MiniMax-
M2.7. We then apply a common threshold of 0.2
to all DeepSeek-V4-Flash experiments, without
separately tuning it for individual verifier variants.
Verifier prompts and additional implementation
details are provided in Appendix D.

SWE-VERIFIERgeneralizes across policy-
model families.All four SWE-VERIFIERvari-
ants improve over the 72.60% no-verifier base-

performs best among the selective variants, reach-
ing 74.27% resolution at 1.13 × cost, while Joint
Rubric reaches 73.93% at 1.08× cost. Two-Stage
Rubric nearly matches trajectory-level LLM-as-
a-Verifier@3, which reaches 74.30% at approxi-
mately 4.19× cost, whereas all-step Qwen3.5-4B
provides no improvement at 2.03 × cost. These
results show that a verifier trained with MiniMax
actions can selectively improve a policy from a
different model family without the cost of uni-
form verification.
All-step verification shortens trajectories but
increases total cost.Executed steps alone under-
state inference cost because verification may gen-
erate and score multiple candidate actions at each
step. All-step Self-Verification yields the fewest ex-
ecuted actions on both policies, reducing MiniMax-
M2.7 trajectories from 46.38 to 38.51 steps and
DeepSeek-V4-Flash trajectories from 41.64 to
33.56 steps. Yet it slightly lowers MiniMax res-
olution and improves DeepSeek by only 0.07 per-
centage points, while total costs rise to 3.35× and

the no-verifier baselines while improving resolu-
tion at much lower cost. Its advantage therefore
comes primarily from avoiding unnecessary candi-
date generation and verification, rather than from
shortening trajectories alone. This distinction mo-
tivates the workflow analysis in Section 6.4, where
we examine whether verification preserves or dis-
rupts the policy’s problem-solving process.

use a lightweight trained model to identify critical
steps and invoke a stronger selector only when its
expected benefit justifies the additional cost.
6.2 Threshold Sensitivity
To examine how verification frequency affects reso-
lution and cost, we vary the trigger threshold from
selective verification to all-step verification. Fig-
ure 3 shows that verifying more steps does not con-
sistently improve resolution. Score Only performs
best at its most selective operating point, reaching
72.28% resolution at 1.02 × cost while verifying
only 0.47% of steps. As its threshold increases and
verification becomes more frequent, resolution de-
clines, eventually reaching 67.16% under all-step
verification. The Untrained verifier is less sensi-
tive to this shift, but its best selective setting still
outperforms its all-step result.
The larger gap between selective and all-step ver-
ification for Score Only suggests that training pri-
marily improves the gate’s ability to identify when
additional sampling is useful; it does not make ev-
ery candidate-ranking decision better. A possible
explanation is a mismatch between training and
inference candidates. Training groups contain di-
verse actions sampled from both weak and strong
models and exclude near duplicates, whereas in-
ference candidates come from the same policy and
may differ only subtly. This mismatch may limit
the trained verifier’s ability to distinguish candi-
dates when it is applied at every step.

Pricing and serving effects.Figure 4(a) shows

benefits more. All-step verification with Qwen3.5-
4B costs 2.1–2.3× and benefits more from caching
because the local verifier adds no API input cost.
Both become more expensive as output prices rise

6.4 Workflow Changes
All-step verification may provide limited gains
when it changes how the policy approaches the task.
We examine this failure mode through verifier-as-
tool, a setting in which the policy knows that a ver-
ifier will select among its proposed actions and is
explicitly prompted to generate diverse alternatives.
Figure 5 shows that ordinary step-level verification
largely preserves the baseline phase structure, with
phase agreement above 93% throughout the trajec-
tory. Verifier-as-tool diverges most during the early
transition from exploration to fixing, and its reso-
lution falls below the baseline despite producing
shorter trajectories. This suggests that verification
is harmful when the requirement to generate al-
ternatives interferes with the policy’s exploration
and repair process. Effective verification should
improve selected decisions while preserving the
policy’s broader workflow. Appendix A reports the
full MiniMax-M2.7 comparison, while Appendix F
analyzes a post-validation final-patch failure.
7 Related Work
Coding Agents.SWE-bench evaluates
repository-level issue resolution (Jimenez
et al., 2024), motivating coding agents built around
interactive tool use, structured repair pipelines,
and trajectory search (Yang et al., 2024; Wang
et al., 2025c; Zhang et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2025;
Antoniades et al., 2025). We study verification of
these agents’ intermediate decisions about what
to inspect, execute, edit, and submit.
Test-time Scaling.Verifiers support test-time
scaling by scoring sampled solutions or interme-
diate steps, including through process supervision
and generative reward modeling (Lightman et al.,
2024; Chen et al., 2025b; Khalifa et al., 2026).
For interactive agents, these scores can assess
progress toward task completion (Xi et al., 2025;
Chae et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Choudhury,
2025). For coding agents, SWE-Gym and R2E-
Gym train agents and verifiers in executable en-
vironments (Pan et al., 2025; Jain et al., 2025);
SWE-TRACE and Guided Search guide search us-
ing rubric-conditioned process rewards or learned
action values (Han et al., 2026; Zainullina et al.,
2025). Other work studies trajectory reranking,
course correction, action-level process rewards,
and execution-free evaluation (Wang et al., 2026;
Gandhi et al., 2025; Dihan and Khan, 2026; Shum
et al., 2025). SWE-VERIFIERcombines a trained
generative action verifier with selective invocation,
using action scores both to identify steps that war-
rant additional verification and to rank candidate
actions before execution.
Inference Efficiency and Selective Verification.
Repeated sampling increases inference cost (Brown
et al., 2024), motivating model cascades, cost-
aware routing, and adaptive compute allocation
(Chen et al., 2023; Ong et al., 2024; Manvi et al.,
2024; Chen et al., 2025a; Liu and He, 2026; Alom-
rani et al., 2025). Coding agents similarly use
budget-aware tool use and efficient scaffolds to
control long-horizon execution costs (Wang et al.,
2025a; Gao and Peng, 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Lin
et al., 2026). Selective verification reduces over-
head by avoiding additional candidate generation
and scoring at every step, saving policy and veri-
fier computation as well as repeated processing of
long trajectory histories. Distilling teacher super-
vision into a smaller verifier further lowers eval-
uation cost (Hsieh et al., 2023). Together, selec-
tive invocation and lightweight scoring target com-
plementary sources of verification overhead: the
frequency of candidate comparison and the cost
of each evaluation.
8 Conclusion
We introduced SWE-VERIFIER, a lightweight ver-
ifier that selectively samples and ranks alterna-
tive coding-agent actions. Trained with execution-
aware teacher supervision, it improves MiniMax-
M2.7 resolution from 75.47% to 77.20% while
verifying 3.43% of steps at 1.09 × total cost. It
generalizes to DeepSeek-V4-Flash, improving res-
olution from 72.60% to 74.27% at 1.13× cost. Our
analyses show that verification works best selec-
tively: a gate targets decisions that benefit from
verification, whereas uniform verification adds sub-
stantial cost without consistent gains. Selective
verification thus improves coding agents while con-
trolling inference overhead.
8

Limitations
Our experiments use the mini-swe-agent harness to
evaluate MiniMax-M2.7 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash
on SWE-Bench-Verified. The observed accuracy–
cost tradeoffs may differ for other coding bench-
marks, repository distributions, serving systems,
or agent frameworks, especially when trajectories
have different lengths or when candidate actions
are not generated from a shared prompt.
Our cost analysis also depends on practical
serving assumptions. Step-level verification is
most favorable when repeated candidate prompts
receive cache discounts and verifier calls are
inexpensive relative to policy calls. Providers with
different cache discounts or batching behavior
may change the break-even point. Finally,
SWE-VERIFIERis trained from teacher-labeled
action preferences and successful trajectory states,
so its judgments may inherit teacher biases and
may be less reliable on failure modes or domains
underrepresented in the training data.

D Verifier Prompts
This appendix records the prompt templates used
for verifier-based candidate selection, critical-step
classification, and teacher-label generation. We in-
clude five verifier configurations. The multi-action
verifier prompt scores a full candidate slate and se-
lects one action. The single-action verifier prompt
uses the same candidate-selection objective but
emphasizes candidate-local reasoning and scalar
scoring. The teacher-distillation prompt exposes
candidate observations or future evidence to the
teacher and asks it to distill that information into
trajectory summaries, checklist criteria, next-state
predictions, checklist scores, and final scalar scores.
The two-stage verifier uses separate prompts to gen-
erate an action-conditioned checklist and score the
proposed action against it. The action-classifier
prompt assigns each sampled action an action cat-
egory, problem-solving phase, uncertainty/risk la-
bels, and a should_run_verifier decision used
for selective verification.
For training, we use the fused AdamW optimizer
with a learning rate of 5×10 −6, cosine learning-
rate decay, and an effective batch size of 16. Train-
ing runs for three epochs on four NVIDIA H200
GPUs using Hugging Face Transformers and Py-
Torch with BF16 mixed precision.

read-only inspection, diagnostics, grep, or tests. Good
search is targeted, relevant, and likely to narrow the next

- plan: direction-setting strategy. Use this when the main
purpose is to choose a concrete next approach,
decomposition, or hypothesis-driven route without mutating

- edit: solution-changing action. Use this when the main

- context_aware: understanding-oriented context

- planning: framing work before substantial diagnosis.
- explore: reducing uncertainty by gathering repository or

- fix: implementing or refining the solution by mutating

gression. Candidate B would reject valid GitHub
URLs without a .git suffix, even though the ex-
isting tests explicitly preserve that behavior. The
verifier therefore captures error-handling preserva-
tion, but misses valid-Git-URL preservation.
Why the checklist verifier matched the reference
decision.The checklist-based verifier reframed
the decision around preservation-constrained
26

progress rather than visible edit progress. Its cri-
terion scores penalize broad overwrites, missing
validation, and regressions to surrounding URL
behavior, while rewarding actions that test the re-
ported Mercurial cases directly. Candidate D scores
highest because it satisfies the immediate validation
criterion without committing the agent to an unsafe
full-file edit. The choice is therefore not a generic
preference for testing over editing; it is a state-
specific preference for non-destructive validation
because the available edit actions carry concrete
regression risks.
General takeaway.This case illustrates how
checklist-conditioned verification can surface la-
tent constraints in next-action selection. The blind
and observation-aware verifiers both choose ac-
tions that look productive under a narrow reading
of the issue. The checklist verifier instead forces
the candidate actions to compete against explicit
preservation criteria already supported by the tra-
jectory. That shift is the key qualitative difference:
the checklist does not merely add more explanation
after the fact; it changes the scoring basis from ap-
parent immediate progress to progress that remains
compatible with surrounding repository behavior.

F.3 Interpretation: final-state consistency
The checkout command is not intrinsically in-
correct. It becomes incorrect here because
cds_parsetab.py is part of the last validated
workspace. At this state, a verifier should judge
final-state consistency: keeping both modified files
preserves the validated state; checking out the
parser table and submitting without revalidation
removes part of the state that made the tests pass;
removing generated files is safe only if the new file
set is revalidated. Earlier exploratory cleanup is
not the issue. The failure is the late transition from
a passing workspace to an unvalidated submitted
patch.
This distinction explains why unresolved runs
can pass tests earlier. Some cleanup actions
are followed by parser-table regeneration and an-
other successful validation. The unresolved out-
come occurs when the final patch is built after
cds_parsetab.py has again been restored or omit-
ted, so the submitted diff no longer matches the
passing state. The higher MiniMax API-call count
should therefore be read as extra post-fix patch-
29

management work, not evidence that the model
never found the repair.
